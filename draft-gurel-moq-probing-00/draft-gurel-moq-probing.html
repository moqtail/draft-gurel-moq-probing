Probe Tracks for Bandwidth Estimation in Media over QUIC Transport

Abstract

This document defines probe tracks for Media over QUIC Transport (MOQT): tracks of synthetic objects, generated on demand, that measure the bandwidth of a single hop. Probe tracks live under reserved, session-scoped Track Namespaces and are never forwarded or cached. The track name selects the shape of the probe: target bitrate, duration or size, object rate, object size, group structure and key-object bursts. For a downlink probe, a subscriber subscribes to a probe track in the namespace (.session, .probe), and the relay generates it, so the subscriber can measure whether a higher bitrate is sustainable on the relay-to-subscriber hop before it switches to a higher-quality track. For an uplink probe, a publisher generates a probe track and publishes it to its relay in the namespace (.session, .sink), to measure the publisher-to-relay hop.¶

1. Introduction

Endpoints of Media over QUIC Transport (MOQT) [MoQTransport] adapt to the network on both sides of a relay. A subscriber runs adaptive bitrate (ABR) logic and has to decide when it is safe to switch to a higher-bitrate alternative of the track it is consuming. A publisher has to tune its encoding parameters dynamically to the uplink network conditions. Both need to estimate the bandwidth of their own hop, and both face the same problem: throughput measured on the current media is limited by the bitrate of that media. An endpoint that sends or receives a 1 Mbps track at 1 Mbps learns only that at least 1 Mbps is available. To learn whether 3 Mbps is sustainable, it has to make more data flow over the hop.¶

This document defines probe tracks: tracks of synthetic objects, generated on demand in a shape described by the track name, that measure the bandwidth of a single hop. Probe tracks are session-scoped: they are never forwarded beyond the hop they measure, and never cached. They come in two directions (Section 2):¶

  • In a downlink probe, a subscriber subscribes to a probe track and its relay generates it, to measure the hop from the relay to the subscriber.¶

  • In an uplink probe, a publisher generates a probe track and publishes it to its first relay, which consumes it, to measure the hop from the publisher to the relay.¶

In both directions, a probe:¶

  • exercises exactly one hop, which is usually the bottleneck for the decision at hand, and adds no load beyond it;¶

  • runs on the same MOQT session and congestion controller as the media, so it sees the same path conditions;¶

  • can imitate a media track (paced objects, groups and key-object bursts) or run as a greedy bulk transfer, depending on what the endpoint needs to learn; and¶

  • uses an object layout similar to the Low Overhead Media Container (LOC) [LOC], so receivers can reuse their LOC parsing logic, and carries a send timestamp on every object, so the receiver can compute throughput, delay variation and loss without clock synchronization.¶

The result of a probe is input to an adaptation decision: for a subscriber, a switch to another track, for example with the mechanism in [TrackSwitching]; for a publisher, a change of its encoding parameters.¶

1.1. Relation to MOQT Padding

MOQT [MoQTransport] already lets an endpoint send padding, on unidirectional streams or in datagrams, to probe for additional bandwidth at a lower priority than Object data, and notes that publishers can use padding to probe without additional subscriptions. Padding is started and shaped by its sender, carries no Objects and no timing information, and is discarded by the receiver.¶

For downlink probing, padding does not fit: the subscriber needs the measurement, but the relay is the sender. The subscriber cannot request padding, choose its rate, shape or duration, or compute delay trends from it. Downlink probes fill this gap.¶

For uplink probing, the publisher is both the sender and the endpoint that needs the result, so padding, together with an estimate from the publisher's own congestion controller, can be sufficient. Uplink probes add the following over padding:¶

  • the relay accepts or refuses the probe before any data arrives;¶

  • the probe has a declared shape that the relay can enforce;¶

  • the probe consists of media-like objects that take the same path through the relay as media; and¶

  • every object carries a send timestamp, so the relay can measure the probe, which pays off once the relay can report its result to the publisher (Section 10).¶

Whether these additions justify uplink probes is an open question (Section 10).¶

1.2. Terms and Definitions

The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT", "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all capitals, as shown here.¶

This document uses the terms Track, Group, Subgroup, Object, Track Namespace, Track Name, Full Track Name, Relay, Publisher and Subscriber as defined in [MoQTransport].¶

Probe Track:

A track of synthetic objects that the probe sender generates for a single subscription, whether the subscription was initiated with SUBSCRIBE or with PUBLISH.¶

Probe Sender:

The endpoint that generates and sends the objects of a probe track: the relay for a downlink probe, the publisher for an uplink probe.¶

Probe Receiver:

The endpoint that receives the objects of a probe track: the subscriber for a downlink probe, the relay for an uplink probe.¶

Probe Requester:

The endpoint that sends the request that starts a probe: the subscriber (SUBSCRIBE) for a downlink probe, the publisher (PUBLISH) for an uplink probe.¶

Downlink Probe:

A probe that a subscriber requests with SUBSCRIBE in the (.session, .probe) namespace, and that the relay generates and sends to the subscriber (Section 9).¶

Uplink Probe:

A probe that a publisher sends with PUBLISH in the (.session, .sink) namespace to a relay, which consumes and discards it (Section 10).¶

Probe Name:

The Track Name of a probe track. It is either a Dynamic Probe Name or a Preset Name.¶

Dynamic Probe Name:

A Probe Name that encodes the probe parameters directly, as defined in Section 5.1.¶

Preset Name:

A Probe Name that the relay maps to a set of probe parameters, as advertised in the probe catalog (Section 9.2). Preset Names are used only for downlink probes.¶

Paced Probe:

A probe sent at a target rate, with objects released on a schedule.¶

Greedy Probe:

A probe sent as fast as the congestion controller and flow control allow, up to the relay's maximum rate, bounded by its size and, optionally, its duration.¶

1.3. Notational Conventions

This document uses the conventions detailed in ([RFC9000], Section 1.3) when describing the binary encoding.¶

As a quick reference, the following list provides a non-normative summary of the parts of RFC9000 field syntax that are used in this specification.¶

x (L):

Indicates that x is L bits long¶

x (i):

Indicates that x holds an integer value using the variable-length encoding as described in ([RFC9000], Section 16)¶

x (..):

Indicates that x can be any length including zero bits long. Values in this format always end on a byte boundary.¶

To reduce unnecessary use of bandwidth, variable length integers SHOULD be encoded using the least number of bytes possible to represent the required value.¶

3. Session-Scoped Namespaces

3.1. Session Namespace

This document reserves the Track Namespace Field .session (eight bytes) as the first field of a session-scoped namespace. A track whose namespace starts with .session belongs to the MOQT session on which it is requested, and is served by the peer at the other end of that session. An endpoint that implements this specification:¶

  • MUST answer a SUBSCRIBE, FETCH, TRACK_STATUS or SUBSCRIBE_NAMESPACE for a namespace that starts with .session itself, and MUST NOT forward it upstream or to any other session;¶

  • MUST consume a PUBLISH for a namespace that starts with .session itself, and MUST NOT forward the track or its objects upstream, to any subscriber, or to any other session;¶

  • MUST NOT share a session-scoped track between sessions or serve it from a cache; and¶

  • MUST NOT announce a namespace that starts with .session to its peers or upstream. A PUBLISH of a session-scoped track goes only to the peer of the session and ends there.¶

Using a dedicated first field lets a relay recognize session-scoped requests with the namespace prefix check it already performs for routing, before any upstream lookup. The leading dot keeps the namespace apart from application namespaces. Probing is the first use of the session namespace; other session-scoped services can be defined in the same way, each with its own second field.¶

3.2. Probe Namespaces

This document defines two session-scoped namespaces, one per direction:¶

(.session, .probe):

Downlink probes. The second field is .probe (six bytes). The only request a probe requester sends in this namespace is SUBSCRIBE (Section 9). A subscriber can subscribe to a probe track without knowing any publisher namespace.¶

(.session, .sink):

Uplink probes. The second field is .sink (five bytes). Messages except PUBLISH MUST NOT be used in this namespace; a relay rejects any other request that names it with REQUEST_ERROR and error code NOT_SUPPORTED. This does not restrict the messages of an established PUBLISH-initiated subscription, such as PUBLISH_OK, REQUEST_UPDATE and PUBLISH_DONE (Section 10).¶

Because the two directions use separate namespaces, the same Full Track Name never names both a downlink and an uplink probe. A SUBSCRIBE in the (.session, .probe) namespace is always served by the relay's own probe generator. A relay MUST NOT treat it as a subscription to a track that the same endpoint publishes.¶

4. Probe Shapes

Probes answer two different questions. This document supports both and expresses them through one set of parameters.¶

Table 2: Probe shapes.
Shape Question it answers Properties
Paced Can the hop sustain X bps right now, alongside the media? Low impact. The flow is application-limited, so it confirms X but does not reveal capacity above X.
Greedy How much headroom does the hop have? High impact and bursty. Capped at the relay's maximum rate. Slow start and congestion window growth affect short probes.

A paced probe can be a smooth stream at a target rate, or it can imitate a video track: objects at a frame rate, grouped into groups of pictures, with a larger first object in each group that stands in for a key frame. The idle time between objects is the same as for real media, so the congestion controller and any pacing on the path behave as they would for the media the probe stands in for.¶

A greedy probe is closer to downloading a segment in HTTP adaptive streaming. It sends a fixed number of bytes, or sends for a fixed time, as fast as possible.¶

5. Probe Names

5.1. Dynamic Probe Names

A Dynamic Probe Name consists of fields separated by -. Each field is a single-letter parameter key followed by a decimal value. The grammar, in ABNF [RFC5234], is:¶

The endpoint that receives the request parses a Dynamic Probe Name by splitting it at each - and reading the first character of each field as the parameter key and the rest as its value. Fields can appear in any order. Probe requesters SHOULD order fields by key, because MOQT compares Full Track Names byte by byte when it detects duplicate subscriptions.¶

The parameter keys are:¶

Table 3: Probe parameter keys.
Key Name Unit Meaning
r Rate bits per second Target payload rate. If absent, the probe is greedy.
d Duration milliseconds Maximum duration of the probe.
b Bytes bytes Maximum total payload bytes of the probe. Required for a greedy probe.
f Object rate objects per second Pacing rate of objects; the frame rate of an imitated video track.
s Object size bytes Base object payload size.
g Group size objects Number of objects per group.
k Key multiplier factor Size of the first object of each group, as a multiple of s.

Examples:¶

d2000-r300000:

A smooth 300 kbps stream for 2 seconds.¶

b500000:

A greedy probe that sends 500,000 bytes as fast as possible.¶

d2000-f30-g30-k5-r3000000:

An imitation of a 3 Mbps video track at 30 frames per second, with one-second groups whose first object is five times the size of the others, for 2 seconds.¶

5.2. Preset Names

A Probe Name that does not match the grammar in Section 5.1 is a Preset Name. Preset Names are used only for downlink probes: the relay looks them up in its table of presets, which it can advertise in the probe catalog (Section 9.2). A relay MUST NOT define a Preset Name that matches the grammar in Section 5.1. For example, hd-check is a valid Preset Name, because hd is not a letter followed by digits.¶

6. Resolving Probe Parameters

The parameters depend on each other, so the probe requester usually gives only some of them, and the rest are derived. The probe sender and the probe receiver both resolve the parameters with the procedure in this section, and get the same result. The endpoint that receives the request resolves them before it accepts the request.¶

Let s be the base object payload size. The first object of each group is ks bytes and the other objects are s bytes, so the mean object size is s(k+g-1)/g. A paced probe therefore has:¶

The defaults for parameters a probe requester omits are fixed by this document, so that both endpoints can resolve any Dynamic Probe Name without asking the other. The default object rate (DEFAULT_F) is 50 objects per second, that is, one object every 20 ms. The default greedy object size (DEFAULT_GREEDY_S) is 16384 bytes. The default key multiplier is 1. When g is absent, the whole probe is one group.¶

A greedy probe MUST give b. Bytes are the natural size of a greedy transfer, like the download of a segment, and they are the resource that the relay has to protect, so the relay can check every greedy probe against its byte limit before it accepts it. A greedy probe MAY also give d as a time bound. A greedy probe has no target rate: it is sent as fast as the hop allows, but never faster than the relay's maximum rate (Section 7.2). A greedy probe without d ends after b bytes, or earlier if it reaches the relay's maximum duration, as described in Section 9 and Section 10.¶

7. Validation, Limits and Errors

7.1. Validation Rules

The endpoint that receives a probe request validates it before it accepts the request with SUBSCRIBE_OK or PUBLISH_OK. The request is invalid if:¶

  • the Probe Name matches neither the grammar in Section 5.1 nor, for a downlink probe, a known Preset Name;¶

  • the Probe Name is a Preset Name in an uplink probe;¶

  • a key is not defined in Table 3, or a key appears more than once;¶

  • a value has a leading zero, does not fit in 62 bits, or is zero for any of r, d, b, f, s, g or k;¶

  • neither d nor b is present, r is absent (a greedy probe) and b is absent, both f and s are present, k is present without g, or f is present without r; or¶

  • a derived value is unusable, for example the resolved object size s is zero because r is too low for the object rate f.¶

An invalid request MUST be rejected with the error code for invalid probes in Section 7.3.¶

7.2. Limits

A relay MUST enforce limits on the resources a probe can use, in both directions. At a minimum, a relay MUST limit the rate, the duration, the total bytes and the object size of a single probe, the number of probes running at the same time on a session, and the total probe bytes per session over a period of time.¶

Per-session limits do not protect the relay's capacity: many sessions, each within its own limits, can still use up the relay's egress capacity with downlink probes or its ingress capacity with uplink probes. A relay MUST therefore also limit, across all sessions and separately for each direction, the number of probes running at the same time and the total rate of probe traffic.¶

The maximum rate applies to every probe. A paced probe whose r exceeds it is rejected. A greedy probe has no r, so the probe sender MUST NOT send it faster than the maximum rate: the relay enforces this directly for a downlink probe, and as part of the declared shape for an uplink probe (Section 10).¶

A per-probe limit (rate, duration, total bytes or object size) that is exceeded can never be met at this relay, so the relay MUST reject the request with the per-probe limit error code in Section 7.3 and a Retry Interval of 0.¶

A session limit (concurrent probes or the per-session byte budget) or a relay-wide limit that is exceeded can be met later, so the relay MUST reject the request with error code EXCESSIVE_LOAD and SHOULD set the Retry Interval to the time after which the request is expected to succeed.¶

The relay MUST NOT silently clamp the parameters, because an endpoint that measures a different probe from the one it asked for draws the wrong conclusion.¶

Unless configured otherwise, a relay SHOULD apply the following default limits. They apply to downlink and uplink probes alike, separately for each direction.¶

Table 5: Default probe limits.
Limit Default
Maximum duration (d) 5000 ms
Maximum total bytes (b) 10,000,000 bytes
Maximum rate (r) 50,000,000 bits per second
Maximum object size (k*s) 1,048,576 bytes
Concurrent probes per session 1
Minimum time between the end of one probe and the start of the next 1000 ms
Probe bytes per session 50,000,000 bytes per 60 s
Concurrent probes across all sessions Set by the relay operator
Total probe rate across all sessions Set by the relay operator

The relay-wide limits depend on the relay's capacity, so this document gives no default for them.¶

7.3. Error Codes

This document defines no new error codes. The endpoint that receives a probe request rejects it with REQUEST_ERROR [MoQTransport], using existing error codes. In a downlink probe the relay is the publisher of the probe track, and in an uplink probe it is the subscriber, so the codes differ by direction:¶

Table 6: Error codes for probe requests.
Outcome Downlink (SUBSCRIBE) Uplink (PUBLISH) Retry Interval
Invalid probe (Section 7.1) DOES_NOT_EXIST UNINTERESTED 0
PUBLISH with Forward State 1 - UNINTERESTED 0
Per-probe limit exceeded DOES_NOT_EXIST UNINTERESTED 0
Session or relay-wide limit exceeded EXCESSIVE_LOAD EXCESSIVE_LOAD time until the request can succeed
Session not allowed to use probes UNAUTHORIZED UNAUTHORIZED 0
Other request in the namespace, such as FETCH NOT_SUPPORTED NOT_SUPPORTED 0

Because one code covers several outcomes, the Reason Phrase carries the detail. It MAY name the field and the limit at fault, for example "r exceeds 20000000". A relay that does not want to reveal its limits MAY send a generic Reason Phrase instead, for example "probe rejected, use a preset". A subscriber that receives DOES_NOT_EXIST for a Dynamic Probe Name can switch to a Preset Name from the catalog, or retry with smaller values; a publisher can retry an uplink probe with smaller values.¶

A relay that does not implement this specification also responds to a SUBSCRIBE in the (.session, .probe) namespace with DOES_NOT_EXIST, or forwards it to a publisher that does the same. A rejected probe request alone therefore does not show whether the relay supports probes; support is discovered separately for each direction (Section 9.2, Section 10.3).¶

8. Probe Objects

8.1. Streams and Priority

The probe sender sends each group on its own subgroup stream with Subgroup ID 0. A greedy probe, and a paced probe without g, is one group on one stream.¶

The probe sender MUST send probe objects with the lowest publisher priority, so that the probe uses only the capacity that is left over after the media on the same session, which is the capacity the probe is meant to measure.¶

8.2. Object Format

Probe objects are similar to objects in the Low Overhead Media Container (LOC) [LOC]: they use the same layout and the same properties, so developers can parse probe objects with the same logic they use for LOC media objects. Probe objects are not LOC media, however: their payload is padding, not an encoded audio or video frame.¶

Each probe object carries the following LOC Public Properties in its MOQT Object Properties:¶

Timestamp (0x10):

The wall-clock time, in microseconds since the Unix epoch, at which the probe sender released the object for sending. The probe sender MUST include it in every probe object. The probe sender MUST NOT send a Timescale property, so that the Timestamp is interpreted as wall-clock time. Probe receivers can use differences between Timestamps without synchronized clocks. Probe receivers whose clock is synchronized with the probe sender can also compute one-way delay.¶

Video Frame Marking (0x09):

For a paced probe with g present, the probe sender MAY include this property and set the independent flag on the first object of each group only, to imitate the key frames of a video track. The probe sender MUST NOT include it otherwise.¶

The payload of a probe object takes the place of the LOC Payload, and consists of padding: bytes that bring the payload to its resolved size (s, or k*s for the first object of a group). The probe sender MUST set them to zero, consistent with the padding defined in [MoQTransport], and receivers MUST ignore their values. Probe objects carry no LOC Private Properties. Because the payload is not media, a receiver MUST NOT pass a probe payload to a media decoder, even if it parses probe objects with a LOC parser.¶

Probe objects carry no other per-object fields:¶

  • The position of an object in the probe follows from its Location. Object i is Group floor(i / g), Object ID i mod g (Section 6). A gap in Locations shows objects that were not delivered.¶

  • The rate and the number of objects follow from the Probe Name, which both endpoints resolve with the procedure and the fixed defaults in Section 6. For a Preset Name, the subscriber resolves the Dynamic Probe Name that the catalog gives for it (Section 9.2).¶

  • The end of the probe is signaled by PUBLISH_DONE.¶

9. Downlink Probes

A downlink probe measures the hop from a relay to a subscriber. The subscriber requests it with SUBSCRIBE in the (.session, .probe) namespace, and the relay generates it.¶

9.1. Requesting a Downlink Probe

A downlink probe is generated for a single subscription. A relay MUST NOT aggregate two subscriptions to the same Probe Name into one generated track, MUST NOT cache probe objects, and MUST NOT serve them to another subscriber. Every probe starts at Group 0, Object 0, whatever the subscription filter. A FETCH for a probe track is rejected (Section 7.3).¶

After SUBSCRIBE_OK, the relay sends the objects according to the resolved schedule. When the probe ends, it ends the subscription with PUBLISH_DONE. The relay sends a greedy probe as fast as the hop allows, but never faster than its maximum rate; a greedy probe without d ends early if it reaches the relay's maximum duration (Section 6). A subscriber can stop a probe early by terminating the subscription.¶

Subscribers SHOULD subscribe to probe tracks with subscriber priority 255, so that, together with the lowest publisher priority (Section 8), the probe measures only the capacity left over after the subscriber's media.¶

Relays SHOULD also accept the following equivalent form: an empty Track Namespace (zero Track Namespace Fields) and a Track Name that starts with the bytes .probe/, followed by a Probe Name. For example, the empty namespace with Track Name .probe/d1000-r300000 is equivalent to the namespace (.session, .probe) with Track Name d1000-r300000.¶

9.2. Probe Catalog

A relay that implements this specification MUST serve a catalog track named catalog in the (.session, .probe) namespace. The catalog uses the Common Catalog Format [CommonCatalogFormat].¶

9.2.1. Catalog as Proof of Support

A subscriber learns whether a relay supports downlink probes by subscribing to the probe catalog. If the subscription succeeds, the relay supports downlink probes. If it fails with DOES_NOT_EXIST, the relay does not, and the subscriber does not send probe requests to it. A rejected probe request alone does not tell the two cases apart (see Section 7.3).¶

9.2.2. Catalog Contents

The catalog lists the relay's presets as tracks. For each preset, the catalog gives:¶

  • name: the Preset Name, which is the Track Name to subscribe to;¶

  • selectionParams: the bitrate (r) and, for a paced probe with an object rate, the framerate (f), so that subscribers can select a preset the same way they select media tracks; and¶

  • probeName: the Dynamic Probe Name that the preset stands for, which lets the subscriber resolve the preset with the procedure in Section 6.¶

The streamingFormat field is set to the custom value probe, and streamingFormatVersion to 1. The probe catalog is read only by subscribers that implement this specification, so a custom value is enough to identify it, and other subscribers never need to parse it. probeName is likewise a custom field. The common track fields set the packaging to loc (Section 8). The catalog does not set namespace, so the tracks inherit the namespace of the catalog track itself. A catalog with no presets has an empty tracks array; it still proves support for Dynamic Probe Names.¶

The catalog MUST NOT carry the relay's limits. Session limits change with the state of the session (Section 7.2), so limits in a catalog would go stale, and a request that fits them can still be rejected. A subscriber learns about limits only from the result of a probe request. For the same reason, a preset in the catalog is not a promise: a relay SHOULD list only presets that fit its per-probe limits, but a request for a preset can still fail with EXCESSIVE_LOAD.¶

When the presets change, the relay publishes the new catalog as a new group.¶

9.3. Downlink Probes Served by Publishers

Any MOQT endpoint that acts as a publisher can serve probe tracks with the same logic as a relay: the same Probe Names, parameter resolution, validation, errors and object format. When a subscriber is connected directly to a publisher, the publisher answers the (.session, .probe) namespace like a relay (Section 3).¶

A publisher MAY also offer probe tracks in its own namespace and list them in its catalog, so that subscribers can reach them through relays and measure the whole path from the publisher. A publisher MUST respond to a FETCH for such a track with REQUEST_ERROR and error code NOT_SUPPORTED.¶

10. Uplink Probes

An uplink probe measures the hop from a publisher to its first relay. The publisher generates the probe and publishes it with PUBLISH in the (.session, .sink) namespace, and the relay consumes it: the relay never caches it and never forwards it upstream or to any subscriber, including the publisher itself (Section 3).¶

A publisher that only needs a sender-side estimate can use MOQT padding instead (Section 1.1). Uplink probes are meant for the cases in which the relay should approve, bound and measure the probe.¶

TBD: Whether uplink probes are needed beyond MOQT padding. Their main advantage over padding, a measurement made by the relay, depends on a way for the relay to report its result (Section 10.4), which is not yet specified.¶

10.1. Requesting an Uplink Probe

The Track Name of an uplink probe MUST be a Dynamic Probe Name (Section 5.1). Preset Names are not used for uplink probes, because the publisher has to resolve the parameters (Section 6) to generate the probe anyway.¶

The publisher sends PUBLISH for the uplink probe track with the FORWARD parameter set to 0, and MUST NOT send any objects until the relay sets the Forward State to 1. The relay validates the request with the rules in Section 7. If the probe is acceptable, the relay sends PUBLISH_OK with Forward State 1, and the publisher starts the probe. Otherwise, the relay rejects the PUBLISH (Section 7.3).¶

Waiting for the relay keeps it in control of what it receives: the relay checks the request against its limits before any probe data arrives. The time at which the publisher receives Forward State 1 is also a clean start time for the measurement.¶

A relay that receives a PUBLISH in the (.session, .sink) namespace with Forward State 1, or without the FORWARD parameter (which defaults to 1), MUST reject it with REQUEST_ERROR, MUST discard any objects already received for it, and SHOULD count those bytes against the session limits. The relay MUST NOT close the session for this reason: a bad probe request affects only the probe, not the media on the same session.¶

10.2. Sending and Enforcement

The publisher sends the probe according to the resolved parameters, with the object format in Section 8; the LOC Timestamp carries the publisher's send time. The publisher ends the probe with PUBLISH_DONE.¶

A relay MUST enforce the declared shape. If the publisher sends more bytes than b, sends for longer than d, or sends faster than r by a margin that the relay chooses, the relay SHOULD pause the probe by sending REQUEST_UPDATE with Forward State 0, or end it by terminating the subscription. A greedy probe has no r, so the relay applies its maximum rate instead, and treats a greedy probe that exceeds it like any other violation of the declared shape; the publisher then knows only that the hop sustained at least the rate it reached. A greedy probe without d that reaches the relay's maximum duration ends: the relay terminates the subscription. The relay discards all probe objects after it has measured their arrival.¶

A relay SHOULD apply the same flow control that it applies to ordinary (e.g., media) streams to uplink probe streams as long as the probe track stays within its pre-defined limits, that is, the parameters in its Probe Name. Flow control is then part of the path being measured, as it will be for the publisher's media.¶

10.3. Support Discovery

TBD: How a publisher learns whether a relay accepts uplink probes. The probe catalog proves support for downlink probes only. Options include a custom root field in the probe catalog, such as "acceptsPublish": true, or a SETUP option that the relay sends when the session is established. A SETUP option is likely the better fit, because it tells the publisher before it sends any request, and it covers relays that do not serve downlink probes.¶

10.4. Measurement Result

The relay receives the probe, but the publisher needs the result. The publisher can estimate the uplink capacity on its own side, from the delivery rate that its congestion controller observes while it sends the probe; a greedy probe is the most suitable shape for this. How much of this information is available depends on the publisher's transport API. MOQT padding (Section 1.1) supports the same estimate.¶

11. Measuring with Probes

This section is informative.¶

The probe receiver measures a probe in the same way in both directions. For a greedy probe, it can estimate throughput as the number of bytes received between the arrival of the first and the last object, divided by the time between those arrivals. Excluding the first object reduces the effect of the request round trip.¶

For a paced probe, the probe receiver compares, for each object, the gap between arrivals with the gap between LOC Timestamps. If the arrival gaps keep growing relative to the send gaps, queuing delay is building up and the target rate is not sustainable. The probe receiver can also compare the receive rate over the probe with r. Gaps in object Locations show objects that were not delivered, for example because the subscription ended early.¶

11.1. Downlink Use

A subscriber that wants to switch from a track at rate R1 to a track at rate R2 can run a paced probe at r = R2 - R1 at the lowest priority while it keeps receiving the current track. If the probe is delivered on time, the path is likely to sustain R2, and the subscriber can switch (see [TrackSwitching]).¶

11.2. Uplink Use

In an uplink probe, the relay is the probe receiver, but the publisher needs the result. A publisher that estimates from its own congestion controller uses a greedy probe and reads the delivery rate at the end of the probe; a publisher that wants to raise its encoding bitrate from R1 to R2 can instead run a paced probe at r = R2 - R1 and use a result reported by the relay (Section 10).¶

12. Security Considerations

A probe request is small, but the probe it starts can carry a large amount of data: from the relay for a downlink probe, and to the relay for an uplink probe. Without limits, an endpoint could use probes to use up relay bandwidth, CPU or memory. Relays MUST enforce the limits in Section 7.2 in both directions, and MAY require that a session be authorized before it can use probes.¶

Greedy probes are the main threat to relay capacity, because they use all the capacity they can get, up to the relay's maximum rate. The relay-wide limits (Section 7.2) keep many simultaneous greedy probes from using up the relay's egress or ingress capacity. Lowest priority makes a probe yield only to the media of its own session, not to other sessions at a shared bottleneck. A relay MAY refuse greedy probes entirely, or accept them only from authorized sessions.¶

For uplink probes, the relay keeps control by requiring Forward State 0 in the PUBLISH and checking limits before it allows any probe data, by enforcing the declared shape, and by counting rejected data against the session limits (Section 10). Relays MAY accept uplink probes only from authorized publishers.¶

Probe data only crosses the hop of the session that asked for it, over a congestion-controlled QUIC connection. Probes therefore cannot be used to reflect or amplify traffic toward a third party.¶

Probe objects expose the probe sender's wall clock, at microsecond resolution, through the LOC Timestamp property: the relay's clock to subscribers, and the publisher's clock to the relay. Probe senders that consider this a concern MAY reduce the clock precision; probe receivers that use only differences between Timestamps are not affected by a constant offset.¶

A relay MAY keep its limits out of Reason Phrases, so that clients cannot use them to fingerprint the relay or tune requests to sit just below the limits. The catalog does not carry limits (Section 9.2).¶
